# MetaGlasses — Pivot: B2B Task-Training Assistant (2026-09-25/26)

**Status: under active discussion, not decided.** Triggered by Meta Connect 2026 (see [[Projects/MetaGlasses/Meetings|Meetings]] for the full dated log of this thread). This note is the standalone reference for the idea itself, the competitive landscape, and the two feasibility investigations run so far — kept separate from [[Projects/MetaGlasses/Todo|Todo]] so the working task list doesn't get cluttered with exploratory research before a direction is chosen.

## Why this is on the table at all

Meta's Connect 2026 keynote made **Muse** — Meta's own personal AI agent — the centerpiece announcement, and it's coming to Meta's AI glasses for hands-free, wake-word, visual-context assistance: "act on what you're looking at." That is the same voice+vision consumer loop this project has been building (Glance), now shipping as Meta's own flagship first-party experience rather than something a third party is uniquely positioned to build. Full Connect findings — DAT 1.0 shipping, App Store distribution still stalled with no GA date, Web Apps gaining text input but not camera/mic — are in [[Projects/MetaGlasses/Meetings|Meetings]]'s 2026-09-25 entry.

Ved's read: a general chat+vision glasses assistant no longer has a clean edge over Meta's own roadmap. The response under consideration is narrower and more B2B: **not** a general assistant, but a task-specific training/assistance tool.

## The idea, in Ved's words

> We would like to create our own recorded demonstrations as reference material and then the VLM would give visual and verbal feedback as well as next steps to the user as they build the thing or recreate the task. The task can be anything, we want this to be a B2B solution that can be suited to a variety of industries, and the training material would ideally come from the owner doing something correctly once. The idea is that this can be used in scenarios where you may be training a worker to build new things or can help as they might make mistakes in the process.

Concretely: an expert records themselves performing a physical task once, via the glasses' camera. That recording becomes reference material. Later, a trainee wearing the glasses attempts the same task; the system watches their live camera feed, compares it against the reference, and gives real-time verbal/visual correction — catching mistakes as they happen, not just narrating generic steps.

**The one-shot premise ("the owner does it correctly once") is the whole differentiator and also the hardest constraint** — it rules out any approach that needs a real labeled dataset per task, which is what most existing prior art (below) actually does.

## Competitive landscape — this is a real category, not empty space

Worth being clear-eyed: "AR work instructions / industrial training assistance" already has funded, shipping competitors — **RealWear** (industrial voice-driven wearables built specifically for this), **Scope AR**, **Taqtile** (Manifest), **Microsoft Dynamics 365 Guides**, **TeamViewer Frontline**. Nearly all of them use a **human remote expert** in the loop for judgment calls, not an autonomous AI — where they do use AI for error detection, it's hand-pose/part-tracking models with an explicit confidence-threshold fallback to a human below ~85% confidence (source: the AI-vs-VLM feasibility research below). Our edge, if this pivot happens, is doing the correction with AI instead of a human on a call, on consumer-grade Meta glasses instead of $2-3k industrial headsets — not "nobody does this."

## Prior art we found directly: `Trvise/VortexLens` (2024, inactive)

A 2024 hackathon/class project (team name "TerraVortex," contributors Aaditya Voruganti and Pranav — people Ved knows of, not personally) attempting a similar idea on **proprietary custom hardware**, not Meta glasses. Read directly from the public repo:

- **Weld defect detection**: YOLOv8s fine-tuned on a full Roboflow-exported dataset (`Weld-quality-inspection-9`, real train/valid/test splits), real-time webcam inference with bounding boxes + confidence.
- **Screw hole detection**: three attempts — classic OpenCV circle-detection heuristics (Hough transforms + circularity math), a Flask MJPEG server streaming the annotated feed to a browser, and a separately-trained YOLO model (`best_screw.pt`).
- **`vision.py`** — the least finished piece: YOLOv5 action-detection paired with raw **GPT-2** (2019-era, not a modern VLM) to generate live text "commentary" on what a worker is doing. Reads as an abandoned early attempt, not something that worked.
- Repo hygiene note: a `secret.json` file is committed and still public in the repo — not opened/exposed by us, flagged only.

**Why this matters**: it's real, working proof that the "fast, purpose-trained detector" architecture (validated independently by the feasibility research below) is buildable — a student project pulled it off in what looks like a few weeks. **But it directly violates our one-shot premise** — their weld and screw detectors both needed real per-class labeled datasets, not one demonstration. Copying their architecture as-is would quietly abandon the thing that's supposed to differentiate this from RealWear/Scope AR/Taqtile.

## Feasibility research #1 — can a VLM reliably catch mistakes at all?

Full research fork, summarized:

- **Verdict: promising, genuinely unproven for reliable fine-grained mistake-catching — not solved, not dead.** A 2026 reality-check paper on real industrial data found current foundation models **not yet ready for autonomous industrial defect/quality decisions** (hallucinated/weakly-grounded outputs the central failure). A GPT-4V anomaly-detection study: good zero-shot/one-shot performance overall, **specifically weak at fine-grained localization of subtle defects**.
- **Architecture must be two-tier, not one VLM watching every frame.** No source runs a frontier VLM per frame — cost alone makes it uneconomical at any real frame rate (Claude Opus ~$0.013/image, GPT-4o ~$0.003/image). Standard production pattern: cheap/fast detector runs continuously; VLM invoked only to explain/verify what that detector flags. Latency itself is fine on the cheap tier (~250-350ms) — this is an economics constraint, not a speed one.
- **Prior art splits into two unmerged lineages**: robotics research (RoboCLIP; a 2025 paper hitting 82.5% success on multi-step tasks from **one demonstration**) proves one-shot learning works for a robot *acting*, with VLM-based verification explicitly flagged as future work, not built. Separately, real industrial AR products ship AI-assisted error detection today using **hand-pose/part-tracking models, not general VLM image comparison**, with a confidence-threshold fallback (below ~85% confidence → route to a human) — worth stealing this fallback pattern regardless of which vision approach we use.
- **Most concrete risk**: factory-floor distribution shift. Hand-pose estimation accuracy drops to 75-85% under real conditions, 15-25% of that error specifically from occlusion — often at the exact moment you need to see the part clearly. Viewpoint mismatch (expert's recording angle vs. trainee's own glasses view) is the same class of problem.
- **Could not verify**: no source describes a shipping product doing our *exact* loop (single video demo → live VLM comparison against a trainee's own glasses camera, no human in the loop at all). Genuinely untested combination, not proven impossible.

## Feasibility research #2 — can the fast tier actually run on-device, on an iPhone, today?

Full research fork, summarized:

- **Verdict: yes, more mature than expected — not an emerging/unproven area.**
- **Concrete answer: YOLOE, specifically the YOLOE-26 variant.** Has a first-class **visual-prompt mode** ("Semantic-Activated Visual Prompt Encoder," SAVPE) — conditions the model on a single reference image for one-shot recognition, purpose-built for exactly "show it one example, then detect that thing." Not a workaround; an explicit supported mode. Lightweight variant is 3.9M parameters. Ultralytics ships an official one-line CoreML export path (FP16/INT8, runs on Apple Neural Engine, iOS 14+), with a dedicated CoreML/mobile deployment guide.
- **Proven, not speculative**: YOLO-World (2024 predecessor, text-prompt rather than image-prompt) already has a real open-source iOS implementation doing real-time camera inference on-device — the CoreML + live-camera pipeline itself is a solved engineering problem independent of which specific model is used.
- **Simpler fallback worth prototyping first**: Apple's own **`VNGenerateImageFeaturePrintRequest`** (Vision framework) — zero setup, first-party, already used at scale on-device (powers Photos' duplicate detection). Coarser (whole-frame "does this look different," not "here's exactly what and where changed") but genuinely one-shot by design and ships with iOS today, no model export step at all. Worth testing as the floor before reaching for YOLOE's added complexity.
- **Known limitation**: one-shot/visual-prompt detection is inherently less accurate than a model trained on a real dataset per class (expected, benchmarked trade-off in the literature) — expect more false positives/negatives than VortexLens's purpose-trained YOLOv8 would give for the same task. This is the real price of keeping the one-shot pitch intact.
- **Could not verify**: no real numbers found on thermal throttling or battery drain from sustained real-time camera-linked inference on iPhone specifically — a real deployment risk to test empirically, not something the literature quantifies yet.

## Where this leaves the architecture (synthesis, not yet built)

⚠️ **Superseded by the 2026-09-27 product framing decisions further down this note** — specifically, item 3 below (human-reviewer fallback) is explicitly rejected. Left as-is here for the historical record of how the thinking evolved; see "Product framing decisions" at the end of this note for what's actually decided.

A candidate shape that satisfies the one-shot premise, informed by both research threads:

1. **On-phone fast tier**: YOLOE (or, as a cheaper first prototype, `VNGenerateImageFeaturePrintRequest`) seeded from the single reference recording, running continuously against the live DAT camera feed — no cloud round-trip for this tier.
2. **Cloud VLM tier**: invoked only when the fast tier flags a deviation, or the user directly asks a question — this is where Claude/GPT-4o/Gemini-class models do the explaining, not the continuous watching.
3. ~~**Confidence-threshold fallback**: below some confidence bar, don't guess — tell the user to ask directly, or (in an enterprise deployment) flag for a human reviewer. Borrowed directly from how Scope AR/Taqtile already handle this.~~ **Rejected — no human-in-the-loop review of any kind. See framing decisions below.**

## Hardware difference from VortexLens — cuts both ways

- **Distribution is much easier for us**: VortexLens needed to build and hand out custom hardware to every user; we need a customer to own or buy Ray-Ban Display glasses, already commercially available.
- **We inherit a real bandwidth ceiling they didn't have**: DAT's camera stream goes glasses → **Bluetooth Classic** → phone, with Meta's own docs describing an automatic quality ladder that drops resolution/frame rate under bandwidth pressure. VortexLens ran a webcam straight into a script on the same machine — no wireless hop at all. Continuous frame sampling at a meaningful rate over DAT is a real open question, not yet validated on real hardware.
- **Compute placement favors us**: DAT frames land on the phone first regardless, so an on-phone fast-detector tier (YOLOE / Vision framework) fits naturally — no backend call needed for the continuous-watching part, only for the occasional VLM explanation call.

## Empirical findings (2026-09-26/27) — three independent approaches tested, all three failed the same way

All test code, images, and a real Foundry API key live in `/Users/vedeti123/Documents/MetaGlasses/GlanceInstruct/` (gitignored `secrets.json`, see `secrets.example.json` for the shape). Real test data throughout — MVTec AD's `screw` category (a controlled industrial anomaly-detection benchmark: a clean reference, a real subtle scratch defect, a real obvious bent-tip defect, and a second genuinely-good screw as a true-negative control) plus real labeled weld images pulled directly from `Trvise/VortexLens`'s bundled Roboflow dataset (`Good Welding`, `Crack`, `Porosity` classes) as a second, independent domain. Every test asked the model/detector to compare a reference against an attempt **blind** — no hint about what kind of defect, if any, to expect.

**1. VLM comparison (Microsoft Foundry, `gpt-4.1-mini`), bare prompt then mitigated.** `GlanceInstruct/compare.py` (bare) and `compare_v2.py` (explicit instruction to ignore camera-angle/lighting/instance variation, plus forced structured JSON output with a 0-100 confidence score). Both real defects were caught correctly in both versions. But **the true-negative case (two different, individually correct parts) produced a confident, detailed, entirely fabricated defect description in every single run** — on screws and independently on welds. Mitigation prompting did not fix it. Worse for the specific question of whether confidence can be used to route/segment: **the false positive scored 90-95% confidence, statistically identical to the genuine true positives.** Self-reported VLM confidence, in this setup, carries no usable signal for trusting a verdict.

**2. Whole-frame similarity (`VNGenerateImageFeaturePrintRequest`, Apple's Vision framework).** `GlanceInstruct/feature_distance.swift` — native, on-device, zero-setup, the same API that powers Photos' duplicate detection. Result was worse than inconclusive: **the ordering was inverted.** A genuinely defect-free screw pair (two different correct individuals) scored a *higher* distance (0.474) than a pair with a real subtle scratch defect (0.397) — the opposite of what any threshold-based rule would need. Same pattern on welds (crack case scored *lower* distance than the good-vs-good pair). Root cause: this is a whole-frame embedding, sensitive to composition/framing/palette, with no concept of a localized region — it can't distinguish "different physical unit, same design" from "an actual defect," because it never looks at *where* something changed.

**3. One-shot object detection (YOLOE-26, visual-prompt mode, on-device-sized 3.9M-param checkpoint).** `GlanceInstruct/yoloe_test.py` — seeded from a single reference image + a bounding box (no dataset, matching the one-shot premise), using detection confidence as the signal. Result was the most concretely disqualifying: **a small, deliberately-more-accurate change to the reference bounding box (computed programmatically via `auto_bbox.py`, not eyeballed) flipped detections on and off and swung confidence by 2-3x**, with no reliable relationship to whether a real defect was present. The obvious screw defect went from "detected at 0.30" to "not detected at all" purely from tightening the box. Too sensitive to a setup hyperparameter to trust as a production signal, at least at this checkpoint size with one reference example.

**Consolidated verdict**: three genuinely different technical approaches (a frontier VLM, a native on-device embedding, a purpose-built one-shot detector), tested against two independent real-data domains, all failed to produce a number or verdict that reliably separates "genuine defect" from "different-but-correct individual instance." This isn't one unlucky method — it's a consistent pattern. It also isn't a surprise in hindsight: it's exactly what the original feasibility research said *before* any code was written — one-shot/few-shot anomaly detection is a known, expected step down from a fully-trained detector (VortexLens's approach) or a human-in-the-loop confidence fallback (what Scope AR/Taqtile actually ship). We've now confirmed that gap ourselves, concretely, three times, rather than only citing it.

**Open framing decision, not yet resolved**: does the product premise loosen (a handful of reference examples instead of strictly one; "reliably catch gross/obvious mistakes, defer subtle ones to a human" instead of promising full autonomous correction) or does this evidence argue for a different pivot shape entirely? Not decided as of this writing.

## Fourth approach tested (2026-09-27) — text-instruction comparison, fails worse than the first three

Instead of comparing a live image against a **reference image**, convert the one-shot reference into a **text description** of what a correctly-performed state should look like (once, up front — mirroring the real proposed pipeline: video in, instructions out), then compare each live image against **only that text**, never the raw reference image, at inference time. Theory: a qualitative description ("the shank should be smooth, no scratches or dents") should be inherently invariant to the camera-angle/individual-instance noise that sank all three image-to-image approaches above, since it never encodes incidental framing/lighting detail to begin with. Test code: `GlanceInstruct/text_instruction_test.py`.

**Result: fails, and surfaces a new risk the other three approaches couldn't have.** The one-time "reference image → text instructions" step is itself unreliable — asked to describe the real weld reference photo, the model generated instructions describing a **screw** ("cylindrical shape with a finely threaded surface... helical grooves"), not a weld bead. It misidentified the object type entirely, plausibly because a weld ripple pattern superficially resembles screw threads. None of the image-to-image approaches could fail this way, since they always had the real reference image available at comparison time to ground against — a lossy one-time text conversion has no such grounding once it's done.

Setting that aside and judging only the screw case (where the generated instructions were accurate and sensible), the core problem persisted **and got worse**: every single test case across both domains — both true negatives *and* both real defects — was flagged as defective, at uniformly high confidence (90-95%). Stripping away the actual reference pixels didn't remove the instance-variation confusion the theory predicted; it removed the one thing letting the model get anything right at all. This is the starkest of the four results: 0% separation between genuine defects and genuine non-defects, worse than the three prior approaches (which at least passed the identical-image sanity check).

**Running tally: four independent architectures (frontier VLM image comparison, native on-device whole-frame embedding, one-shot object detection, text-instruction comparison), tested across two independent real-data domains, all four have failed to separate "genuine defect" from "correct-but-different individual instance."** The pattern has not broken once. This is no longer "one unlucky method" — it's a consistent, reproduced finding across meaningfully different technical strategies, and it reinforces (rather than just theoretically predicts) that the one-shot premise as currently scoped is the actual hard constraint, not an implementation detail to engineer around.

**Open framing decision, still not resolved**: loosen the product premise (a handful of reference examples instead of strictly one; scope down to "reliably catch gross/obvious mistakes, defer subtle ones to a human" instead of promising full autonomous correction), or treat this as evidence the pivot needs a different shape entirely. Four failed technical approaches is a strong enough signal that further tweaking the detection method itself has diminishing returns — the next useful move is probably this framing conversation, not a fifth architecture.

## Follow-up test (2026-09-27) — does asking for reasoning before the verdict help?

Tried adding a `reasoning` field to the JSON schema, filled in **before** `has_defect` (so it drives the verdict rather than rationalizing an already-decided one). Only meaningful for the two methods that are actually language models — `compare_v3.py` (image-to-image) and `text_instruction_test_v2.py` (text-instruction). The other two (`feature_distance.swift`, YOLOE) produce a bare number/bounding box with no capacity to explain themselves at all; not applicable, not a gap to close.

**Image-to-image reasoning genuinely helped, on this run.** The screw true-negative case — a clean false positive in `compare_v2.py` — **flipped to correctly saying "no defect,"** with the reasoning explicitly catching itself: *"the apparent small dark spot... appears to be a shadow or surface detail rather than a defect."* Both real defects stayed correctly flagged with specific, accurate reasoning. The one remaining failure (weld true negative) is still wrong, but the reasoning text is legible enough that a human skimming it could plausibly catch the mistake — it's describing two visibly different weld *jobs*, which a person would question in a way the raw JSON verdict alone never surfaced.

**Text-instruction reasoning did not help, and reconfirmed the instruction-generation bug is repeatable, not a fluke.** Re-run instruction generation produced *different* wrong wording this time (a Torx drive instead of Phillips; "helically ridged cylinder" instead of "threaded shank" for the weld reference) — the object misidentification recurs with different specific details each time, meaning it's a systematic weakness of this step, not a one-off. All 5 verdicts were still `has_defect: true` at uniform 95% confidence, same as without reasoning. Worse from a human-trust standpoint: the reasoning text here reads detailed and confident even when built on wrong instructions, which could mislead a reviewer rather than help one — the opposite of what happened with the image-to-image method.

**Caveat, not yet resolved**: this is one run per case. LLM outputs have real run-to-run variance; "reasoning fixed the screw true-negative" is a promising signal worth taking seriously, not yet a proven, repeatable fix — would need several more runs per case to know if this holds up. **Net effect on the framing decision**: reasoning-before-verdict looks like a real, if partial, mitigation specifically for the image-to-image architecture, and specifically as an aid to a human-in-the-loop reviewer (matching the Scope AR/Taqtile confidence-fallback pattern) rather than as something that makes the raw verdict trustworthy on its own.

## Round 2 (2026-09-27) — all four methods re-tested on 5 new, previously-unused screw images

The single-run caveat above prompted a real follow-up: 5 new MVTec AD screw images never used in any prior round (2 more true negatives — different individual good screws — plus 3 new, previously-untested defect types: `thread_top`, `scratch_head`, `thread_side`), run through all four methods against the same `reference_good.jpg`. Test code: `compare_v3_round2.py`, `feature_distance_round2.swift`, `yoloe_test_round2.py`, `text_instruction_test_v2_round2.py`.

**Image-to-image + reasoning (`compare_v3`) went 5/5 correct — and that makes it 8/8 correct on screws across both rounds combined.** Every true negative correctly said no defect; every real defect (three of them never tested before) was correctly flagged with specific, accurate reasoning. This is no longer "one lucky roll" — it's a real, repeated result. The only failure this method has produced anywhere remains the single weld true-negative case from round 1 (thin sample — only 2 weld pairs tested total, versus 8 screw pairs now).

**Vision framework feature distance got further disconfirmed, not just inconclusive.** Both new true negatives (0.461, 0.365) scored a *higher* distance than all three new real defects (0.358, 0.340, 0.334) — the inversion from round 1 held up and got cleaner with more data. Any threshold you could set would flag the correct screws and miss the defective ones. This method is now confirmed unusable, not merely unproven.

**YOLOE produced zero detections across all 5 new images** — a complete non-result, weaker even than round 1's brittle on/off behavior. At this checkpoint size (26n, 3.9M params) and with a single reference example, it isn't engaging with this domain reliably enough to be a signal at all.

**Text-instruction + reasoning improved but didn't reach image-to-image's reliability: 4/5 correct**, one false positive (a genuinely good screw flagged as defective — "irregular threads... damaged screwdriver slot" that isn't actually there). Notably, the instruction-generation step correctly identified the object as a screw both times this round (the weld object-misidentification bug from round 1 didn't recur here) — but the false positive shows the method still isn't as reliable as having the real reference image available for direct comparison.

**Updated overall verdict**: reasoning-augmented image-to-image VLM comparison is now the clear leading candidate for the fast-check tier, with real repeated evidence behind it (8/8 on screws), not a single favorable trial. It is not yet validated on welds (2/2 pairs tested, 1 wrong) or on any domain beyond these two, and the single-reference-example (true one-shot) premise still hasn't been stress-tested against a genuinely large, diverse image set — 8 correct calls is meaningful, not exhaustive. Next real step, if pursued further: test on a third, different object category to see if the screw result generalizes, and/or run several more weld pairs specifically to see if that domain's single failure was representative or a fluke in the other direction.

## Round 3 (2026-09-27) — a genuinely different third category (hazelnuts), and a real nuance surfaces

Deliberately chose a domain unlike either prior one: MVTec AD's `hazelnut` category. Not another manufactured metal part — individual hazelnuts vary naturally in shape, color, and cap position far more than a machined screw does, making "real defect vs. normal instance variation" a harder test in principle. 2 true negatives (different individual good hazelnuts) + 3 real defect types (`crack`, `cut`, `hole`), all four methods, against `hazelnut_reference_good.jpg`. Test code: `compare_v3_hazelnut.py`, `feature_distance_hazelnut.swift`, `yoloe_test_hazelnut.py`, `text_instruction_test_v2_hazelnut.py`.

**Both LLM-reasoning methods went 5/5 — image-to-image is now 13/13 on screws + hazelnuts combined (welds remain 1 of 2, from round 1), and text-instruction reached its best showing yet (5/5, up from 4/5 on screws and 0/5 on welds in earlier rounds).** Reasoning correctly attributed shape/orientation differences between the two individual hazelnuts to natural variation in both true-negative cases, across both methods, and correctly identified all three real defects with accurate descriptions.

**The two non-LLM methods behaved completely differently here than on screws/welds — this is the important new finding, not just "another domain, same result."**

- **Vision framework feature distance flipped to the *correct* ordering.** Both true negatives (0.367, 0.306) scored *lower* distance than all three real defects (0.496, 0.395, 0.456) — a clean, threshold-able separation (anywhere around ~0.38-0.40 would perfectly sort all 5 cases). This is the opposite of the inverted ordering found on screws and welds.
- **YOLOE produced real detections this time** (unlike the complete null result on screws round 2) **but still no reliable ordering**: both true negatives scored high confidence (0.77, 0.87) as hoped, but two of the three real defects (cut: 0.90, hole: 0.93) scored *just as high or higher* — only the crack case showed the expected lower confidence (0.31). Mixed, not usable.

**Working theory for why domain matters this much**: hazelnuts in this dataset are consistently centered, similarly scaled, and similarly oriented against a plain dark background across every photo — much more consistent framing than an elongated screw photographed at a random diagonal angle, or a weld bead where every photo is an entirely different piece of equipment. Whole-frame methods (Vision framework, and to a lesser/messier extent YOLOE) seem to depend heavily on that framing consistency to work at all — when object pose/scale is naturally controlled across the reference and every attempt, a whole-frame embedding has less incidental noise to confuse with a real defect. When it isn't controlled (screws at arbitrary rotation, welds as completely different scenes), the same method inverts or goes silent.

**This is a real, actionable product implication, not just a research curiosity**: if the actual reference-recording process controls for consistent camera framing/distance/angle (plausible — a real "record the demo" flow likely has the person position the camera similarly each time, the same way MVTec's hazelnut photos are consistently centered), then the **cheap on-device methods might become viable after all**, not just the VLM-reasoning ones — this hasn't been true in any test so far, but every prior test used a domain with inconsistent framing (screws at arbitrary rotation, welds as different scenes entirely). Worth a deliberate follow-up test: hold framing consistent on purpose (not just whatever a stock photo dataset happened to do) and see if Vision-framework/YOLOE reliability improves further, since that would directly inform whether the on-device fast tier is salvageable with a framing constraint on capture, versus needing the cloud VLM call for every check.

**Updated running tally**: image-to-image + reasoning, 13/13 on screws + hazelnuts and 1 of 2 on welds (14/15 overall) — the strongest, most repeated result of anything tested. Text-instruction + reasoning, improving domain by domain (0/5 → 4/5 → 5/5) but with a documented instruction-generation failure mode (misidentified a weld as a screw in round 1) not yet re-triggered but not disproven either. Vision framework and YOLOE: both are domain-dependent rather than uniformly broken, with framing consistency as the likely deciding factor — worth a targeted test before fully ruling either out.

## Product framing decisions (2026-09-27) — Ved's direction after reviewing all three rounds

Real product/scope decisions, not just technical findings — recorded as instructed, distinct from the exploratory synthesis above.

- **Keep the "convert reference material to text instructions" step in the architecture. Do not drop it**, despite it underperforming image-to-image comparison in every round tested so far (0/5 → 4/5 → 5/5, always behind or level with image-to-image's 13/13). Ved's call, stated reasoning: "I think end of the day this will be useful" — a product-level bet on the text-instruction layer paying off, not something the technical results argued for on their own. Both approaches stay in play; this isn't a decision to stop testing text-instructions, just a decision not to discard it because image-to-image is currently winning.

- **No human-in-the-loop review, of any kind.** This explicitly rejects the "confidence-threshold fallback to a human reviewer" idea floated in the architecture synthesis above (which was itself borrowed from how Scope AR/Taqtile actually operate) — Ved does not want that pattern here. **The maximum acceptable fallback under uncertainty is showing the reference material itself on-screen** (as pictures) so the trainee can self-compare — no routing to a supervisor, no human review step of any kind, automated or otherwise. This is a real, meaningful point of difference from the RealWear/Scope AR/Taqtile competitive set discussed earlier in this note, which lean on exactly the human-in-the-loop pattern being rejected here.

- **Defer on-device inference entirely, for now.** The framing-consistency lead from round 3 (Vision framework/YOLOE) is not being pursued immediately — explicitly deferred, not abandoned. Consistent with the earlier reasoning that continuous on-device monitoring may not even be necessary for a v1 (checkpoint-triggered cloud calls could suffice), though Ved's decision here is simply "not now" rather than tied to that specific reasoning.

- **Continue viability testing using the existing cloud setup** (Microsoft Foundry, `gpt-4.1-mini`, `/Users/vedeti123/Documents/MetaGlasses/GlanceInstruct/`) rather than standing up anything on-device during this validation phase.

## New open technical question (2026-09-27, not yet tested) — does image-to-image comparison scale to a full video reference, not just one photo?

Ved's question, posed directly: what if the reference material is an entire **video** to follow (his example: a YouTube video) rather than a single photo — can pure image-to-image comparison "keep up"? This is a genuinely different test from anything run in rounds 1-3, which only ever compared **one static reference photo against one static attempt photo** — a single frame, not a multi-step recording. A real task recording has many steps changing over time; a single reference frame can't represent all of them. This is likely the strongest concrete argument *for* keeping the text-instruction layer (per the decision above): a whole video is naturally harder to reduce to "one comparable image" than a single object photo was, whereas compressing a video into step-by-step text instructions is a much more natural fit for that kind of source material. Not yet tested — the next thing to validate.

## Scope confirmed beyond cooking (2026-09-27)

Cooking (the boiled-eggs guide) has been the validation vehicle purely because it's easy to test solo — the actual intended domains explicitly include **construction/building** and **car inspection & maintenance**, confirmed directly by Ved. Those domains have real recurring patterns cooking doesn't exercise: repeated-fastener checklists (torque all N lug nuts), numeric specs beyond just timing (torque values, fluid quantities, tolerances), instrument/gauge reading, and materially higher safety stakes. A feature backlog for these was written into `PLAN.md` Part 8: count-based checklists (in progress), generalized numeric-spec extraction, gauge reading, non-stereo measurement (reference-object scaling recommended over ARKit-on-phone or VLM-estimation, given the glasses-only hands-free constraint), a free-form "what am I looking at" mode, and automatic safety flagging — the last one explicitly flagged as higher-risk and not to be built without a deliberate discussion first, given it edges into liability-sensitive advice in a way the rest of the product doesn't.
